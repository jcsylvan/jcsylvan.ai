# Part Two. Point of Law: USML Category VIII, Aircraft and Related Articles

Curriculum Theme B, topic seven. The index named Category VIII as the next topic and asked this memo to read the proposed removal of paragraph (h)(10) beside the current text, to take up the 2013 first Export Control Reform rule that wrote the category, the 2026 aircraft survivability equipment amendments, the Category IV(b) cross reference, and the temporary note to paragraph (h)(1), and to treat the carrier rule in Note 2 to Category II(a) as introduced. Each is taken up below, and the temporary note turns out to have been terminated more than a year ago.

## Question

The hypothetical prime's aerospace unit has four matters on its desk. A foreign government wants a business jet fitted with a directed infrared countermeasures system to carry its head of state, and asks what the aircraft and its maintenance will require after the October 2026 rule. A foreign fighter program with an active electronically scanned array fire control radar wants to buy parts the hypothetical prime designed for the F-35 and other parts the hypothetical prime will design for the foreign aircraft alone. A customer wants to convert a retired attack helicopter to fight wildfires and asks whether the stripped airframe leaves the United States Munitions List (USML). Procurement asks about a low probability of intercept radar altimeter, a wing fold for a gas turbine aircraft with hard points outboard of the fold, an unmanned aircraft with a 350 kilometer one way range, steel and carbon brake pads, tires, and a part common to the F-16 and the F-35. Trade compliance asks what Category VIII enumerates, how the significant military equipment (SME) and Missile Technology (MT) markings fall, how Export Control Classification Number (ECCN) 9A610 picks up the rest, how brokering applies, and what changed in 2025 and 2026.

## Rule

USML Category VIII at 22 CFR 121.1 controls thirteen populated classes of aircraft in paragraph (a), numbered to sixteen with three reserved,, shipboard launch and recovery equipment in paragraph (d), Department of Defense funded developmental aircraft in paragraph (f), twenty-two populated classes of parts, components, and systems in paragraph (h), and directly related technical data and defense services in paragraph (i), with SME markings on paragraphs (a)(1) through (a)(5), (a)(7), (a)(8), (a)(15), and (h)(20), and MT where an unmanned aircraft, or the aircraft or missile a part serves, has a range of 300 kilometers or more (binding law). Every other aircraft specially designed for a military use, and every other specially designed part, is subject to the Export Administration Regulations (EAR) under ECCN 9A610, which places the aircraft in 9A610.a, thirty-three classes of low sensitivity parts in 9A610.y, and the remainder in 9A610.x (binding law). The current text reflects the September 15, 2025 rewrite of paragraphs (h)(1) and (h)(29), which also terminated the temporary KF-21 note, and the October 13, 2026 exclusion of survivability-enhanced aircraft from paragraph (a)(8), while the October 1, 2026 proposed rule would remove and reserve paragraph (h)(10) radar altimeters (binding law and proposed rule).

## Analysis

**Assumptions.** This memo assumes that none of the aircraft or parts carries a commodity jurisdiction determination, that the articles are U.S. origin unless stated, that no destination is listed in 22 CFR 126.1, and that the foreign fighter program entered production after 2023. It reaches no classification conclusion. Each result turns on design history, certification, and integration facts that trade compliance holds and this memo does not.

**Where the text stands.** The eCFR displays Title 22 as of November 24, 2026, and Category VIII reads as set out below. \[12\] Four rules shape it.

The first Export Control Reform rule, 78 FR 22740 (April 16, 2013), FR Doc. 2013-08351, RIN 1400-AD37, effective October 15, 2013, rewrote the category after a November 7, 2011 proposed rule drew thirty-one comments (binding law). \[13\] The revised category narrows the aircraft on the USML to those that warrant control under the Arms Export Control Act, moves gas turbine engines to the new Category XIX and ECCN 9A619, marks Missile Technology Control Regime items with the MT parenthetical, and replaces the generic parts control with a positive list, with one principal exception for parts specially designed for the low observable aircraft then listed in paragraph (h)(1). The Department declined to retain the old note to paragraph (h), known as the section 17(c) note on common parts, because the specially designed definition would do the same work, and it confirmed that 120.41(b)(3) performs the function of that note across the USML. It also narrowed the computer control in (h)(16) to systems with a purely military function or specially designed for a Category VIII or 9A610 aircraft. \[13\]

The targeted revisions rule, issued as an interim final rule at 90 FR 5594 (January 17, 2025), FR Doc. 2025-01313, and adopted with changes at 90 FR 41778 (August 27, 2025), FR Doc. 2025-16382, effective September 15, 2025, rewrote paragraph (h)(1) into four listed classes, revised (h)(29), moved the note at the end of the category to a new Note 1 to paragraph (h), and added the F-47, MQ-25, and RQ-170 to the aircraft list (binding law). \[14\] \[15\] It defined foreign advanced military aircraft in 121.0 as a non-U.S. origin aircraft, including a foreign derivative of a U.S. aircraft, in development or entering production after 2023, with an active electronically scanned array fire control radar, integrated signature management, integrated electronic warfare systems, or the ability to engage targets beyond visual range, and the final rule explains that a weather radar does not meet the first criterion and that a federated electronic warfare system does not meet the third (binding law and agency guidance in the preamble). \[15\] The interim final rule states that it supersedes and terminates the temporary modification of the note to paragraph (h)(1) published at 88 FR 84072 (December 4, 2023) and extended at 89 FR 93170 (November 26, 2024), which had kept parts used in the KF-21 within (h)(1), and it chose 2023 as the production year in the new definition to align with that modification (binding law). \[14\] \[16\] The index therefore needs correcting. The December 1, 2026 end date in the extension rule never arrived because the modification ended on September 15, 2025, and the KF-21 is now reached, if at all, through (h)(1)(iii) and the 121.0 definition. \[16\]

The aircraft survivability equipment rule, an interim final rule at 91 FR 55457 (August 28, 2026), FR Doc. 2026-17660, RIN 1400-AG17, docket DOS-2026-0892, effective October 13, 2026, revised paragraph (a)(8) to exclude survivability-enhanced aircraft, defined ASE, secured ASE, survivability-enhanced aircraft, and USML in 121.0, and added paragraphs (a)(8) and (a)(9) to 120.54 (binding law). \[17\] The DDTC web notice gives October 12 as the effective date and the rule text gives October 13, so the rule text governs. \[7\] \[17\] Comments closed September 28, 2026 and no final rule has appeared. \[1\] \[7\]

The October 1, 2026 proposed rule, 91 FR 62361, FR Doc. 2026-20079, would remove and reserve paragraph (h)(10), which the Department says would take most radar altimeters off the USML because they no longer provide a critical military or intelligence advantage, and it amends nothing else in Category VIII (proposed rule). \[18\] Comments are due November 30, 2026.

**What the category enumerates.** Paragraph (a) covers aircraft, whether manned, unmanned, remotely piloted, or optionally piloted, in thirteen populated classes numbered to sixteen: bombers, fighters and fixed wing attack aircraft, turbofan or turbojet trainers for those pilots, attack helicopters, unmanned aerial vehicles specially designed to incorporate a defense article, aircraft specially designed to incorporate a defense article for intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance, for electronic warfare other than survivability-enhanced aircraft, for airborne warning and control, for command, control, and communications, and for air refueling, target drones, aircraft capable of in-flight refueling, roll-on/roll-off airlifters that carry 35,000 pounds over 2,000 nautical miles into short or unimproved fields other than the L-100 and LM-100J, U.S. aircraft with original military designations A, B, E, F, K, M, P, R, or S and their foreign equivalents, and armed aircraft or aircraft specially designed as a platform to deliver munitions. The MT parenthetical reaches unmanned aircraft with a range of 300 kilometers or more, and Note 2 defines range as one way distance on the most fuel efficient profile with no reserve. Note 1 sends every other military aircraft to 9A610, including unarmed aircraft built before 1956 and unmodified since, and treats safety of flight and other Federal Aviation Administration modifications as leaving an aircraft unmodified. SME asterisks mark (a)(1) through (a)(5), (a)(7), (a)(8), and (a)(15). \[12\] Paragraph (d) covers launching and recovery equipment specially designed to let a paragraph (a) aircraft operate from a Category VI vessel, which the 2013 preamble confirms excludes fixed land based arresting gear. \[13\] Paragraph (f) covers Department of Defense funded developmental aircraft and their specially designed parts, with notes excluding aircraft in production, aircraft sent to the EAR by a commodity jurisdiction determination, and aircraft the contract identifies as dual application, and applying only to contracts dated April 16, 2014 or later. \[12\]

Paragraph (h) lists the parts. Paragraph (h)(1) reaches parts specially designed for the B-1, B-2, B-21, F-15SE, F/A-18E/F, EA-18G, F-22, F-35, F-47, F-117, MQ-25, RQ-170, and future variants, and for U.S. Government technology demonstrators, excluding parts common to unlisted aircraft that are or were in production, and its paragraphs (iii) and (iv) keep those parts in (h)(1) when they are reused in a foreign advanced military aircraft or a Category XXI(a) aircraft. Note 1 to (h)(1) gives the F-16 and F-35 example, and Note 2 gives the technology demonstrator example. The other populated paragraphs are high speed rotorcraft gearboxes with thirty minute loss of lubrication capability (h)(2), tail boom and rotor folding (h)(3), wing folding for Category IV(d) powered aircraft or for gas turbine aircraft meeting one of six criteria (h)(4), on-aircraft arresting gear (h)(5), bomb racks, launchers, rails, pylons, and external stores systems with MT above 300 kilometers (h)(6), which is where Note 1 to Category IV(b) sends launcher mechanisms for aircraft, damage adaptive and threat adaptive flight controls (h)(7) and (h)(8), non-surface effectors (h)(9), the radar altimeters in (h)(10), air refueling systems (h)(11), swarming flight control systems with MT (h)(12), lift fans and roll posts for short take-off vertical landing aircraft (h)(14), integrated helmets with weapon cueing (h)(15), fire control and stores management computers (h)(16), mission and vehicle management computers specially designed for Category VIII aircraft (h)(17), drive and flight control systems designed to survive a 7.62 millimeter hit (h)(18), in-flight thrust reversers (h)(19), classified items with the SME asterisk (h)(20), variable speed gearboxes (h)(27), high output electrical power and thermal management systems for Category XIX engines (h)(28), and scale models, iron bird rigs, and tooling specially designed for (h)(1) articles, the listed aircraft, or paragraph (f) aircraft (h)(29). Note 1 to paragraph (h) lets the Department of Commerce license the (h)(3) through (h)(5), (h)(7), (h)(14), (h)(17), and (h)(19) items when they are incorporated in a 9A610 aircraft, while replacement parts stay under the ITAR. Paragraph (i) covers technical data and defense services, including classified technical data for ECCNs 9A610, 9B610, 9C610, and 9D610, and paragraph (x) carries EAR items on a Category VIII license. \[12\]

**The CCL side.** ECCN 9A610, read today on the BIS posting of Part 774 rather than the eCFR, controls in paragraph .a military aircraft specially designed for a military use that are not enumerated in Category VIII(a), with the LM-100J named, with trainers, cargo and utility aircraft, military helicopters, observation aircraft, balloons, and unarmed military unmanned aircraft as examples, and with an exclusion for aircraft first manufactured before 1946 that carry no USML article or operable weapon (binding law). \[19\] Paragraph .b covers L-100 aircraft built before 2013, .e through .j cover mobile arresting systems, refueling and confined area equipment, aircrew life support, parachutes, and military ground effect machines, and .t through .w carry the MT items for unmanned aircraft: composite structures and launch and handling equipment for 300 kilometer aircraft, and radar altimeters, flight control systems, and servo valves for aircraft that can carry 500 kilograms to 300 kilometers, with the lesser versions falling to .x. Paragraph .x takes the remaining specially designed parts for 9A610 commodities and Category VIII defense articles, and .y lists thirty-three low sensitivity classes, among them aircraft tires (y.1), steel brake wear pads other than sintered or carbon materials (y.19), lead acid and nickel cadmium batteries (y.24), unarmed military aircraft built from 1946 to 1955 (y.29), and brackets, cables, chargers, mounts, and propellers for 9A610.a unmanned aircraft (y.33). National security and regional stability controls apply in Column 1, except for .b, for .x parts going into a .b aircraft, and for .y, which carries regional stability controls only to China, Russia, and Venezuela under 742.6(a)(7). MT applies to .t through .w and to 9A610.a unmanned aircraft with a 300 kilometer range regardless of payload. License Exception LVS applies at \$1,500, STA paragraph (c)(1) is barred for 9A610.a absent a 740.20(g) determination, STA is barred for MT aircraft with a 500 kilogram payload to 300 kilometers even to Country Groups A:5 and A:6, and STA paragraph (c)(2) is barred for the whole entry. The related controls point to Category XIX and ECCN 9A619 for engines and to ECCN 0A919 for foreign military commodities with more than de minimis 600 series content. \[19\]

**The head of state jet.** Before October 13, 2026, a business jet modified to carry a directed infrared countermeasures system was an aircraft specially designed to incorporate a defense article for an electronic warfare function under (a)(8), and the Department's preamble confirms it was described there even with the equipment removed, so every maintenance task on the airframe, down to EAR brakes, was a defense service (binding law and agency guidance). \[17\] The aircraft now falls outside (a)(8) if it is a survivability-enhanced aircraft under 121.0: a manned aircraft that was in production, was described only in ECCN 9A991.b, held a Federal Aviation Administration type certificate as a transport or small commuter airplane or an equivalent certificate from a North Atlantic Treaty Organization member or major non-NATO ally, was modified only to incorporate secured ASE, and is not described in any other USML paragraph. Secured ASE covers only directed infrared countermeasures and the infrared or ultraviolet missile warning systems needed to run them, installed with anti-tamper measures. \[17\] If those facts hold, the aircraft is a 9A991.b item, while the countermeasures system remains a Category XI(a)(4) defense article inside it under 120.11(c). Servicing the system, and the dedicated wiring and power supplies installed for it, remains a Category XI(d) defense service, as does helping the foreign operator modify the airframe for the system before it is installed, while maintaining the rest of the airframe is no longer a defense service. Under 120.54(a)(8) the aircraft may enter the United States temporarily with the system aboard without an authorization, provided neither the aircraft nor the system passes to another foreign person here, and under 120.54(a)(9) the operator may fly the system from one foreign country to another, but only if the system left the United States under a license rather than an exemption, and a retransfer abroad remains a controlled event (binding law). \[17\] The open fact is anti-tamper. The definition does not say who confirms that the measures deter, delay, and detect access, so the hypothetical prime should document the measures and ask trade compliance whether an advisory opinion or commodity jurisdiction request is warranted before the first unlicensed movement (commentary).

**The foreign fighter parts.** A part the hypothetical prime designed for the F-35 stays in (h)(1) when the foreign program reuses it, because (h)(1)(iii) was written to stop a (h)(1) part from being released through later use in a foreign advanced military aircraft, and the Department says the purpose was to ease licensing of such reuse (binding law and agency guidance). \[14\] \[15\] A part the hypothetical prime designs only for the foreign aircraft is outside (h)(1), because the paragraph lists the aircraft whose parts it reaches and the foreign aircraft is absent from that list, and the final rule rejects the reading that (h)(1)(iii) creates a catch-all for foreign fighter parts (agency guidance). \[15\] Such a part is 9A610.x unless another (h) paragraph or another USML category describes it, and a scale model or iron bird rig for the whole foreign aircraft is (h)(29) while a rig for one of its components is outside (h)(29). \[15\] The part common to the F-16 and the F-35 is outside (h)(1) under Note 1 to (h)(1) and goes to the 120.41 analysis, which will usually land it in 9A610.x or, if it is on the .y list, in 9A610.y (binding law). \[12\] Two readings are open on the words in development, or entering production, after 2023 in the 121.0 definition. On the first, a foreign aircraft whose production began during 2023 is outside the definition, and reuse of (h)(1) parts in it would release them under 120.41(b)(3) once that aircraft is in production. On the second, the Department's stated purpose of aligning with the KF-21 modification, which concerned an aircraft whose production began in 2023, shows that 2023 programs were meant to be inside the definition. The text favors the first reading and the preamble the second, and the judgment is JC's (commentary). \[14\]

**The wildfire helicopter.** Paragraph (a)(4) enumerates attack helicopters without the words specially designed, so the release paragraphs of 120.41(b) do not apply, and the question is whether the airframe remains an attack helicopter after its weapons, hard points, and mission systems are removed. The October 1, 2026 proposed rule answers in the preamble to proposed 120.35. A helicopter designed to deliver munitions stays in Category VIII even when used only to fight wildfires, because the airframe structure that carries the stub wing loads was designed for munitions and removing the weapons and hard points leaves that structure in place, in contrast to the Category VII(b) launcher chassis example the November 26 memo taught (proposed rule). \[18\] Until that rule is final the preamble is the Department's reading of existing law, and under existing law the safer course for an enumerated aircraft is a commodity jurisdiction request under 120.12, because the customer will otherwise be asked to prove a negative about airframe design (commentary).

**The procurement items.** A radar altimeter with low probability of intercept power management or signal modulation is (h)(10) today, with MT if it serves an unmanned aircraft or missile of 300 kilometer range (binding law). \[12\] If the proposed rule is finalized as written, the paragraph is reserved and the altimeter is classified on the CCL, where 9A610.v already controls radar altimeters for unmanned aircraft with a 500 kilogram payload to 300 kilometers with MT, and 9A610.x takes the rest (proposed rule and binding law). \[18\] \[19\] The Department says most rather than all radar altimeters would leave, and an altimeter that is classified or specially designed for an (h)(1) aircraft would remain in (h)(20) or (h)(1). The wing fold for a gas turbine aircraft with hard points outboard of the fold is (h)(4)(ii)(D). The 350 kilometer unmanned aircraft is (a)(5), SME, and MT if it is specially designed to incorporate a defense article, and it is 9A610.a with MT if it is unarmed and carries no defense article, with range measured under Note 2 to paragraph (a) (binding law). \[12\] \[19\] Steel brake wear pads are 9A610.y.19, carbon pads are 9A610.x, and tires are 9A610.y.1, and the .y items reach a Category VIII aircraft as well as a 9A610 aircraft because the paragraph names defense articles in Categories VIII and XIX (binding law). \[19\] A gun integrated into an aircraft is controlled with the aircraft under Note 2 to Category II(a), taught on November 22, and the pylons and launchers that carry its munitions are (h)(6). \[12\]

**Brokering.** Section 129.4(a)(2)(v) requires prior DDTC approval for brokering activities involving aircraft and unmanned aerial vehicles described by Category VIII, without paragraph limits, so the requirement reaches every U.S. origin Category VIII aircraft and unmanned aircraft unless an exemption in 129.5 applies, and 129.4(a)(1) reaches any foreign Category VIII article (binding law). \[20\] Whether (a)(2)(v) also reaches a stand alone paragraph (h) part turns on whether the words aircraft and unmanned aerial vehicles describe the whole category or only paragraph (a), and the text supports both readings (commentary). \[20\]

**What this memo did not reach.** The 121.0 definition of aircraft, the Wassenaar Munitions List entry ML10, ECCNs 9B610, 9C610, 9D610, and 9E610, the BIS companion rule of April 16, 2013, the USML Category VIII text at 27 CFR 447.21, the December 4, 2023 temporary modification rule itself, and the eCFR text of 9A610 were not read today.
